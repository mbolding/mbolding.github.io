fNIRS Simulator

A sibling to the MRI, PET, EEG and MEG simulators. Describe a recording in a few lines, shine near-infrared light through scalp, skull and cortex, catch what comes back, and turn it into oxy- and deoxyhaemoglobin. Then let the heartbeat, the breath, blood-pressure waves, the scalp, a loose optode and the room lights in, and do what an fNIRS lab does: check, correct, filter, regress, average and model. The simulator always knows what the cortex really did, so every answer can be checked.

Working
The idea: shine light in, catch what comes back

Skin, bone and brain are nearly transparent to near-infrared light between about 650 and 950 nm, but they scatter it fiercely: a photon changes direction every tenth of a millimetre. Light shone into the scalp at one point therefore diffuses through the head, and a little of it, a billionth or so, comes back out a few centimetres away. On its way it passes through the cortex, where oxyhaemoglobin and deoxyhaemoglobin absorb it, each with its own colour. When neurons fire, blood flow rises and the mix changes, and so does the light that comes out. An fNIRS system is a set of light sources at two or more wavelengths, optodes held against the scalp by a cap, detectors sensitive enough to see a few picowatts, and a way of multiplexing so each detector knows which source its light came from. Light sources emission against the haemoglobin spectra

Source

A laser diode emits a line a nanometre or two wide; an LED spreads over 30 to 50 nm. The conversion to haemoglobin uses the extinction coefficients at the nominal wavelength, but an LED's photons sample the spectrum across its whole band, weighted toward wherever haemoglobin absorbs least. Where the spectrum is steep (below 750 nm for deoxyhaemoglobin) the effective coefficient differs from the nominal one by several percent, and the error leaks between HbO and HbR. Lasers need fibres and safety interlocks; LEDs can sit on the scalp, which is why most wearable systems use them. Either way the power at the skin is kept to a few milliwatts per square millimetre.

Optodes, the cap and coupling the most common reason a channel fails

The cap holds sources (squares) and detectors (circles) in a fixed layout over a 10-20 position. A channel is a source-detector pair close enough to see each other's light, usually 3 cm apart, and its signal comes from the tissue between them. The small circles are short-separation detectors, 8 mm from a source, that see almost only scalp.

Hair

Light has to get from the optode tip into the skin and back out at the detector. Hair in between blocks it, scatters it and, if dark, absorbs it: melanin absorbs more strongly at shorter wavelengths, so dark hair costs more light at 760 nm than at 850. Each optode is pushed through the hair and wiggled into place, which is why setting up a cap takes longer than recording with it. The numbers are the light reaching a 3 cm detector through two optodes, relative to bare skin.

The tissue what light passes through on its way to the cortex and back

Head
atnm

Absorption coefficient μa (how often light is absorbed) and reduced scattering coefficient μs′ (how often it effectively changes direction) of each layer, computed from its blood, water and background content. Scattering is a hundred times stronger than absorption everywhere except in the cerebrospinal fluid, a clear layer through which light channels sideways. The Light in tissue tab follows photons through these layers.

Detectors and dynamic range light falls about tenfold for every centimetre

Detector

The detected power at each separation for a 10 mW source (adult head, 850 nm, bare skin). An 8 mm channel and a 4 cm channel on the same detector differ by four orders of magnitude, so a detector must handle microwatts without saturating and picowatts without drowning in its own noise. A photodiode is linear over a huge range but noisy; an avalanche photodiode multiplies its photocurrent internally; a silicon photomultiplier counts single photons but saturates early, which makes short channels hard. The shaded band is where a detector gives a usable signal (signal-to-noise above 100 at 10 Hz).

Multiplexing: telling the sources apart animated

Continuous wave, frequency domain and time domain homogeneous tissue, diffusion theory

Continuous wave measures only how much light arrives. Many pairs of absorption and scattering give the same intensity (the curve), so CW cannot tell them apart and reports only changes, through a pathlength it must assume (the DPF). It is cheap, simple and the basis of most fNIRS systems, and of this simulator.

Frequency domain modulates the source at around 100 MHz. The light arrives smaller (demodulated) and late (phase shifted); the phase is the mean time of flight, so it measures the pathlength, and with intensity it separates μa from μs′ to give absolute concentrations.

Time domain sends picosecond pulses and times each returning photon. The distribution of times of flight holds everything: its decay gives μa, its rise μs′, and photons that arrive late have travelled further and deeper, so gating on late photons rejects the scalp. The most informative, and the most expensive.

A recording is a description

Every fNIRS study can be written down as a handful of decisions: which wavelengths, how fast to sample and how to multiplex; where the probe goes and how its sources and detectors are laid out, including short channels; whose head it is; what the cortex does and when; what the body does anyway; what goes wrong; and how the data will be analysed. The program on the left is exactly that, in a small language. Change a line and press Build recording: the simulator records a new session and every other tab updates. The examples are a good place to start. The language is described under the editor.

      Why the light path is a banana, and why that is a trade-off
      
    Each photon takes a random walk: a straight step, a new direction, another step, until it is absorbed or escapes. Most escape close to the
        source. The few that reach a detector centimetres away must have wandered deep enough to get there and back, and the region they visited, weighted
        by how likely each route is, is shaped like a banana between source and detector. Its depth grows with separation, roughly a third to a half
        of it, but every extra centimetre costs about a factor of ten in light. A short channel sees the scalp; a 3 cm channel reaches the cortex but
        still spends most of its path in scalp and skull; a 5 cm channel goes deeper still but gets almost no light. The orange paths are individual
        photons from a Monte Carlo random walk; the shaded banana is the sensitivity from diffusion theory, which the photons fill in as they accumulate.

      Two unknowns need two equations
      
    A change in light at one wavelength could be a change in oxyhaemoglobin, a change in deoxyhaemoglobin, or any mix of the two. Measure at a
        second wavelength where the two absorb in a different ratio and you have two equations in two unknowns, which can be solved. How well depends on
        how different the ratios are. Below the isosbestic point near 800 nm, deoxyhaemoglobin absorbs more; above it, oxyhaemoglobin does.
        One wavelength on each side gives well-separated equations; two on the same side give nearly parallel ones, and the solution amplifies noise and
        mixes the two. Below 650 nm haemoglobin absorbs so strongly that little light survives; above 950 nm water does. Between lies the
        near-infrared window.

      From light to haemoglobin in three steps
      
    The instrument reports intensity. Taking minus the logarithm of the intensity relative to its baseline gives the
        optical density change, which for small changes is proportional to the change in absorption along the path. The
        modified Beer-Lambert law says how: ΔOD(λ) = [εHbO(λ) ΔHbO + εHbR(λ) ΔHbR] × d × DPF(λ),
        where d is the source-detector distance and the differential pathlength factor says how much longer than d the light really travelled.
        With two wavelengths that is two equations, solved for ΔHbO and ΔHbR. A continuous-wave system cannot measure the DPF, so it is assumed,
        and the law also assumes the change fills the whole path, when in fact it is confined to a patch of cortex that the light only grazes.

      What the probe can see
      
    Each channel is sensitive to a banana-shaped volume, and only the deep end of the banana reaches the brain. A patch of active cortex shows up in
        a channel only as much as the two overlap: move the activation sideways and it leaves one channel and enters another; push it deeper and it fades
        from all of them; thicken the skull and the whole brain recedes. The colours on the probe are each channel's sensitivity to the region, the
        centimetres of mean path that lie inside it. Drag the region on the probe, or use the sliders, then press Apply to record with it.

      Everything that is not the cortex
      
    The light that reaches a detector has crossed the scalp twice, and the scalp is full of blood vessels that pulse with every heartbeat, swell
        with every breath and wave with the blood pressure. Worse, the scalp's blood flow responds to the task itself, through the blood pressure, so its
        changes are time-locked to the stimulus and look like activation. On top of the body come the instrument's problems: optodes that move, slip or
        barely touch the skin, and light from the room. Each is switchable here without changing anything else in the recording, so you can see its
        fingerprint alone or in company. The other tabs update as you switch.

      Finding the response in the noise
      
    A single trial's response is a fraction of a micromolar, smaller than the heartbeat and the Mayer waves. Averaging trials time-locked to the
        stimulus shrinks everything that is not time-locked by the square root of the number of trials, so the canonical shape emerges: HbO rising over
        five or six seconds, HbR falling by a third as much, both returning after the stimulus ends. What is time-locked survives averaging, including the
        scalp's response to the task. The GLM does the same job with a model, fitting a predicted response shape to the whole time series at
        once, which copes with overlapping trials and can include the short channel as a regressor.

      One neurovascular event, two measurements
      
    fNIRS and BOLD fMRI watch the same thing: active neurons trigger a rise in blood flow larger than the rise in oxygen use, so the blood in
        and downstream of the active cortex becomes more oxygenated. More oxygenated blood means more HbO and less HbR. fNIRS measures both changes
        directly, optically. fMRI measures HbR indirectly: deoxyhaemoglobin is paramagnetic and dephases the MR signal, so less of it means more
        signal. The same balloon model drives both here, from one neural event: flow, blood volume and oxygen use in, HbO, HbR, total haemoglobin
        and BOLD out. Then each modality samples it in its own way.

      From channels to images: diffuse optical tomography
      
    A sparse probe with one separation gives one number per channel: a map at best (topography), blurred over centimetres and
        with no depth. A high-density array overlaps many channels at several separations, so every point in the tissue is seen by many
        bananas of different depths. Writing the sensitivity of every channel to every voxel as a matrix, the measurements are that matrix times the
        absorption change, and inverting it, with regularization because there are far more voxels than channels, gives a three-dimensional image.
        Short separations pin down the shallow part, long ones the deep part. This is what modern HD-DOT systems do with hundreds of channels.

    What the simulation computes
    
      
      
        
    • Optical properties. Each of five layers (scalp, skull, CSF, gray and white matter) has a blood, water and background content;
          absorption follows from Prahl's tabulation of the haemoglobin extinction coefficients and Hale and Querry's water absorption, and reduced scattering
          from a power law in wavelength. The values give adult differential pathlength factors of 6 to 7 at 3 cm, near the measured ones.
      • 
        
    • Light transport. The diffusion equation for a point source on a layered half-space is solved by finite volumes on an axisymmetric
          grid (0.5 mm cells), with the partial-current boundary condition at the surface. By reciprocity one solution per wavelength gives the sensitivity of
          every source-detector pair, J = G(s,x) G(x,d) / G(s,d), whose integrals are the partial pathlengths. The Monte Carlo on the Light tab is an
          independent, isotropic-scattering random walk in the same layers, used for the pictures and as a check.
      • 
        
    • Haemodynamics. Neural activity drives the balloon model (Buxton, Wong and Frank; Friston and colleagues): flow, venous volume,
          deoxyhaemoglobin and oxygen use, with a flow / oxygen-use coupling ratio and an optional initial dip. HbO, HbR and BOLD all come from it.
      • 
        
    • The recording. Every contribution (each cortical region, each systemic signal, each artifact) is turned into a change in log
          intensity through the channel's partial pathlengths, and the instrument adds coupling, ambient light after demultiplexing, detector noise and
          saturation. Every stochastic part has its own random stream, so switching one off changes nothing else.
      • 
        
    • Analysis. Optical density from the mean intensity; scalp coupling index (Pollonini), coefficient of variation, saturation and
          signal level for quality; TDDR (Fishburn) and spline interpolation (Scholkmann) for motion; zero-phase Butterworth filters; the modified Beer-Lambert law
          with fixed, age-dependent (Scholkmann and Wolf) or modelled DPF; short-channel regression subtracted first or estimated jointly with the task;
          block averaging; and a GLM with a canonical or gamma response, cosine drifts and optional AR prewhitening (as in AR-IRLS, without the robust weights).
      • 
        
    • Tomography. The sensitivity matrix of a high-density array over a voxel grid at one wavelength, and a Tikhonov-regularized
          minimum-norm reconstruction with optional depth compensation.
      • 
      
    
      
    What it does not model
    
      
      
        
    • A real head. The tissue is flat layers under a small probe, so the curvature of the head, the folding of the cortex and the varying
          thickness of the CSF are absent; a deep activation stands in for one in a sulcus.
      • 
        
    • Photon transport in detail. Diffusion theory is approximate within a few millimetres of the optodes and in the clear CSF; the Monte Carlo uses
          isotropic scattering. Neither matters for the ideas here, and both are much faster than a full transport code.
      • 
        
    • Real physiology. The heartbeat, breathing and Mayer waves are realistic in frequency and size, but their spread across the layers is a
          set of fixed weights, and the scalp's response to the task is a simple smoothed copy of the task.
      • 
        
    • Instrument specifics: detector gain switching, source drift with temperature, crosstalk between channels, and the exact timing of any real system.
