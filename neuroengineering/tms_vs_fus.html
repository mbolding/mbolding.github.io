TMS vs. Focused Ultrasound

Two non-invasive ways to push on a circuit through an intact skull, one mature and shallow, one focal and deep. Companion page to TMS and FUS.

Key takeaway: TMS induces an electric field that directly drives axons to threshold, so it produces action potentials you can measure in a muscle within milliseconds, but Maxwell's equations impose a depth versus focality trade that no coil design escapes. Low-intensity FUS delivers mechanical energy that a phased array can steer to a millimeter-scale spot anywhere in the brain, including targets TMS can never reach selectively, but it mostly biases excitability rather than forcing spikes, its mechanism is still contested, and the human evidence base is roughly where TMS was in the early 1990s.

Side by side

Property TMS Low-intensity FUS
Energy delivered Electromagnetic. A 1 to 2.5 T pulse with a 50 to 100 microsecond rise time induces an electric field of roughly 100 V/m in cortex. Mechanical. An acoustic pressure wave, typically 0.1 to 1.5 MPa peak rarefactional at the focus.
Carrier dB/dt through a coil, no carrier frequency in the usual sense. 200 kHz to 1.5 MHz, with 250 to 700 kHz typical for transcranial work.
Skull Effectively transparent. The skull does not perturb the magnetic field. The hard problem. It attenuates, refracts, mode-converts and defocuses the beam, and it heats.
Coupling None needed. Coil sits on the scalp, hair is fine. Gel, a water bladder or a coupling cone. Hair degrades transmission and often has to be parted or wetted through.
Focal spot About 1 to 2 cm across at the cortical surface with a figure-8 coil, and it spreads with depth. Roughly 2 to 5 mm lateral by 10 to 30 mm axial, a cigar aligned with the beam. Set by the wavelength and the aperture.
Useful depth Gyral crowns and nearby cortex, on the order of 2 to 3 cm. Deep coils trade focality for a few more centimeters. Anywhere, including thalamus, amygdala, insula and brainstem, with the intensity peak at the focus rather than at the surface.
Effect on neurons Suprathreshold. Depolarizes axons, preferentially at bends and terminals, and evokes spikes. Mostly subthreshold. Biases excitability, apparently through mechanosensitive channels and membrane mechanics.
Direction of effect Reasonably well mapped. Roughly, 1 Hz and cTBS suppress, 10 to 20 Hz and iTBS facilitate. Poorly mapped. Excitation versus suppression depends on duty cycle, pulse repetition frequency and target in ways that are not yet predictive.
Temporal precision Sub-millisecond. Good enough for chronometry and virtual-lesion timing experiments. Tens to hundreds of milliseconds per sonication. Fine for state changes, poor for single-event timing.
Direct readout Motor evoked potentials give a same-session dose-response curve and a subject-specific threshold. No equivalent. Effects are inferred from fMRI, EEG, evoked potentials or behavior.
Works inside an MRI Awkward at best. Large artifacts, special hardware, rarely done. Yes, routinely. Concurrent sonication and fMRI, and MR thermometry and MR acoustic radiation force imaging for targeting.
Targeting workflow Neuronavigation to an anatomical or functional target, then set intensity relative to motor threshold. Neuronavigation plus an acoustic simulation on a CT or MR-derived skull model to predict where the focus actually lands.
Main confound A loud click and scalp and facial nerve stimulation. Sham coils are imperfect. Skull-conducted sound at the pulse repetition frequency can drive auditory cortex and mimic a neural effect.
Regulatory status Cleared for depression, OCD, smoking cessation and related indications. Thousands of clinical systems in service. High-intensity thermal ablation is approved for tremor. Low-intensity neuromodulation remains investigational.
Cost and footprint A clinical system is roughly the price of a nice car and rolls between rooms. A benchtop single-element research rig is comparable. An MR-guided phased array helmet is a capital project.

Why the physics diverge at the skull

  • TMS ignores the skull and pays for it in depth Faraday's law, not conduction.
    • Because the coil induces the field rather than injecting current, skull resistivity is irrelevant. That is exactly why TMS reaches threshold where tDCS cannot.
    • The cost is that the induced field is set by the coil geometry alone, and it falls off steeply with distance. Making the hot spot deeper means making it wider, because you can only add depth by using larger current loops that are farther from the target.
    • This is the depth versus focality trade. Simulation studies across dozens of coil designs show it is a hard frontier, not an engineering gap. Deep coils such as the H-coil and the double cone do reach further, but they stimulate a much larger volume on the way in, so the deep target is never stimulated selectively.
  • FUS fights the skull and buys arbitrary depth Aberration correction, not geometry.
    • Bone is faster, denser and far more absorbing than brain, and its thickness varies across the head. A wavefront crossing it arrives smeared unless each element of the array is delayed to compensate.
    • With a CT-derived or MR-derived skull map and per-element phase correction, a large array can put a clean focus deep in the brain. Single-element transducers, which most neuromodulation labs use, cannot correct aberration and rely on simulation to estimate the resulting focal shift, which is commonly a few millimeters.
    • Frequency is the central design knob and it cuts both ways. Lower frequency crosses the skull with less attenuation and less aberration, but the focal spot scales with wavelength, so you give up resolution to get transmission.
    • Because the beam converges, intensity peaks at the focus rather than at the surface. That is the property TMS cannot match, and it is the whole reason FUS is interesting for subcortical targets.

What actually happens to a neuron

This is the deepest difference between the two, and it is why the two literatures do not look alike.

  • TMS: a known transducer Field gradients drive axonal membranes.
    • The induced electric field depolarizes axons where the field gradient along the fiber is largest, which in practice means bends, branch points and terminals rather than somata.
    • Because the mechanism is understood, the dose has a natural unit. Intensity is expressed relative to each subject's motor threshold, and the motor evoked potential provides a direct check that the machine is doing something to the brain.
    • Repetitive protocols then work through activity-dependent plasticity, with after-effects that resemble LTP and LTD and outlast the session.
  • FUS: several candidate transducers, none settled Mechanical coupling to the membrane.
    • Leading candidates are direct gating of mechanosensitive channels such as Piezo1, TRP family channels and the two-pore potassium channels TREK-1, TREK-2 and TRAAK, along with acoustic radiation force, stable cavitation within the lipid bilayer, and changes in membrane capacitance.
    • These are not mutually exclusive and their relative weight probably differs by cell type, which is one reason effects reverse sign across parameter sets that look similar on paper.
    • Without a motor evoked potential equivalent, there is no quick positive control. A session can fail silently, and the field has not converged on a dose unit that transfers between labs.

The dials you actually set

TMS

  • Coil type: figure-8 for focality, double cone or H-coil for depth.
  • Intensity as a percent of resting or active motor threshold.
  • Pattern: single pulse, paired pulse for intracortical inhibition and facilitation, or repetitive trains.
  • Frequency and pattern for rTMS: 1 Hz, 10 to 20 Hz, or theta burst in continuous or intermittent form.
  • Pulses per session and sessions per day. Accelerated protocols compress a six-week course into days.
  • Coil orientation, which changes the induced current direction and materially changes the response.

FUS

  • Fundamental frequency, which sets skull transmission and focal size together.
  • Pressure or derated intensity at the focus, reported as spatial-peak pulse-average and spatial-peak temporal-average intensity.
  • Pulse repetition frequency and duty cycle, the two parameters most often blamed for flipping excitation to suppression.
  • Tone burst duration and total sonication duration, from a 100 ms burst during a task to tens of seconds for offline protocols.
  • Focal depth and steering, plus the aberration correction or simulation that justifies believing the focus is where you say.
  • Thermal budget for skull and brain, which constrains everything above.

Note the asymmetry. The TMS list is a short menu of protocols with published dose-response behavior. The FUS list is a continuous parameter space where the mapping to physiological effect is still being charted, which is why the ITRUSST consensus statements on standardized reporting and biophysical safety matter so much for reading the literature.

Safety and who you cannot study

  • TMS Well characterized after four decades.
    • Seizure is the serious risk and it is rare, on the order of one event per tens of thousands of sessions under published guidelines, with risk concentrated in people with epilepsy, certain medications or sleep deprivation.
    • Common and minor: scalp pain, facial twitching, headache, and transient hearing effects from the click, which is why hearing protection is standard.
    • Excluded: ferromagnetic hardware near the coil, cochlear implants, some stimulator hardware. Consensus safety and reporting guidelines are mature and widely followed.
  • FUS Good record so far, thinner denominator.
    • The two limits to respect are thermal rise, which is often worst in the skull rather than the target, and mechanical effects from cavitation, bounded in practice by conventions inherited from diagnostic imaging and now refined by the ITRUSST safety consensus.
    • Reported adverse events in human low-intensity studies are mild and mostly transient, headache, sleepiness, scalp sensation and neck pain, but total human exposure is orders of magnitude below TMS, so absence of rare events is not yet strong evidence.
    • Excluded: skull plates, shunts and craniotomy hardware in the beam path, and beam paths that cross sinuses or thick, highly variable bone. Low skull density ratio is a known predictor of poor coupling for high-intensity work.

The confound each field has to argue about

Both modalities are noisy in a way that can masquerade as a neural effect, and in both cases the fix is the same in spirit, an active control that reproduces the peripheral sensation without the intended brain effect.

  • TMS delivers a loud, sharp click plus direct stimulation of scalp muscle and trigeminal afferents. Subjects can tell real from sham, and the click alone can entrain auditory responses in EEG.
  • FUS pulsed at an audible pulse repetition frequency drives the cochlea through bone conduction. Rodent work showed that a substantial part of what looked like direct neuromodulation was an indirect auditory response, which forced the field to adopt smoothed pulse envelopes, masking sound and deaf-animal controls.
  • Practical consequence for reading papers: ask what the control condition was before asking how large the effect was. This applies to both literatures, but it is more often unresolved in the FUS one.

Evidence and maturity

  • TMS A clinical tool with a research use.
    • In routine clinical service for treatment-resistant depression since 2008, with later clearances including OCD, smoking cessation, anxious depression and adolescent depression, plus accelerated functionally-targeted theta-burst protocols.
    • Large randomized trials, registries and meta-analyses exist. The open problems are effect size, responder prediction and the well-documented variability of after-effects between individuals, not whether the device does anything.
  • FUS A research tool with a clinical future.
    • Human low-intensity work began in earnest in the 2010s with sensory and motor cortex studies, then extended to deep targets. Non-human primate work showing effects that persist for roughly an hour after a short sonication is a large part of why the field is optimistic.
    • Studies are typically small, parameters vary widely, and replication across sites is the current bottleneck. Keep the ablation and blood-brain-barrier applications separate in your head when reading claims about approval status, since those are different intensity regimes with different regulatory histories.
    • Worth noting, the high-intensity thermal use of the same physics is already approved and routine for essential tremor and tremor-dominant Parkinson's, which means the hardware, targeting and safety engineering have been through the clinic even though the low-intensity neuromodulation indication has not.

Choosing between them

Reach for TMS when

  • The target is cortical and reachable from the surface.
  • You need spikes, not a bias, for example a virtual lesion or a corticospinal probe.
  • You need millisecond timing relative to a stimulus or a response.
  • You want a per-subject dose readout, which means anything anchored to motor threshold.
  • The work is clinical, or has to be defensible to an IRB with minimal novel-device burden.

Reach for FUS when

  • The target is subcortical or deep, thalamus, amygdala, insula, striatum, brainstem.
  • You need the deep target modulated without modulating the cortex above it.
  • You want millimeter-scale spatial specificity, including distinguishing adjacent nuclei.
  • You want to stimulate and image at the same time inside an MRI.
  • You are pursuing something TMS cannot do at all, such as localized blood-brain barrier opening or sonogenetic approaches.

They are not really competitors. TMS is the right instrument for cortical causal questions and for a clinic today. FUS is the right instrument for deep causal questions and is the plausible non-invasive alternative to DBS for some indications tomorrow. The honest summary of the current state is that TMS knows what it does but cannot reach very far, and FUS can reach anywhere but is still establishing what it does.
