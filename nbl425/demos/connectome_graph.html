Topological Principles of Brain Networks

1. From Streamlines to Adjacency Matrix:

A structural connectome models the macroscale wiring of the brain. Whole-brain tractography generates millions of streamlines. By mapping cortical gray matter into parcellated Regions of Interest (ROIs), we count the number of streamlines connecting region \(i\) to region \(j\), forming the symmetric adjacency matrix \(A_{ij}\). Thresholding removes false-positive streamlines resulting from tracking errors in crossing-fiber zones.

2. Small-World Architecture:

Random networks have short path lengths but negligible clustering. Lattice networks have high clustering but long path lengths. Brain networks exhibit small-world organization: high local clustering \(C \gg C_{\text{rand}}\) (neighboring regions are densely interconnected for specialized local processing) combined with short characteristic path lengths \(L \approx L_{\text{rand}}\) (long-range association fasciculi enable rapid whole-brain communication), producing \(\sigma = (C/C_{\text{rand}}) / (L/L_{\text{rand}}) > 1\).

3. Rich-Club Phenomenon:

A subset of high-degree brain nodes (including the precuneus, superior frontal cortex, posterior cingulate, and thalamus) are more densely connected to one another than expected by degree distribution alone. This "rich club" forms a central high-capacity backbone for integrating information across disparate functional domains.

4. Attack Vulnerability & Clinical Pathology:

Brain networks demonstrate a classic "scale-free-like" resilience profile: highly robust against random injury, but extraordinarily vulnerable to targeted attacks. In Alzheimer's disease and traumatic brain injury, amyloid deposition and axonal shearing disproportionately affect metabolic hubs, rapidly degrading global network efficiency:

\[ E_{\text{glob}} = \frac{1}{N(N-1)}\sum_{i \neq j} \frac{1}{d_{ij}} \]
