What this figure is for

This is the figure to draw on the board when someone asks why we bother with a 180° pulse. The fan on the left is the whole argument: dephasing is ordering, not loss, and order can be reversed.

1/T2* = 1/T2 + γΔB0   →   echo(n·TE) = M0 e−n·TE/T2

  • Run the train and watch the peaks, not the wiggles. The signal between echoes is T2*-limited and tells you mostly about your shim. The peaks are T2-limited and tell you about the tissue. A multi-echo sequence fits an exponential through those red dots, which is exactly how a T2 map is made.
  • Press FID only. With no refocusing you see the raw T2* decay, and the signal is gone long before T2 has run its course. The gap between that curve and the dashed envelope is what the 180° pulses buy back.
  • The inhomogeneity slider does not move the peaks. Push it to 20 Hz: the fan opens faster, the signal between echoes vanishes sooner, and the echo peaks stay exactly where they were. That independence is the entire point of the spin echo, and it is why spin-echo T2 values are reproducible across scanners while T2* values are not.
  • Why TE controls contrast. Set T2 to 300 ms and then 1500 ms, and compare the height of the last echo. Long-T2 tissue such as CSF still has signal at a long TE while short-T2 tissue has none. That difference is T2 weighting.

Careful with the cartoon: the isochromats here keep a constant frequency, so refocusing is perfect. Real spins diffuse between regions of different field and molecular tumbling randomises their phase. That randomness is precisely T2, and it is what stops the echo train from going on forever.
