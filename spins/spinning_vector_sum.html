What this figure is for

A voxel almost never contains one substance. Protons in fat are shielded by their surrounding electrons, so they feel a slightly smaller field and precess about 3.4 ppm slower than protons in water. Two vectors, two rates, one sum, and the sum depends on when you choose to measure it.

Δf = 3.4 ppm × f0   |   TEopposed = 1/(2Δf)   |   TEin = 1/Δf

  • The beat is the whole point. At TE = 0 the two species add. Half a beat later they subtract. The readouts give both times for the current Δf, and the dashed lines on the plot give both amplitudes. At 3 T the first opposed-phase echo lands at about 1.15 ms and the first in-phase echo at about 2.3 ms, which is why those two numbers appear on abdominal protocols.
  • Chemical-shift imaging falls out of this. Take one image at each TE and you can solve for the two species separately: (in + opposed)/2 is water, (in − opposed)/2 is fat. That is two-point Dixon, and it is why an adrenal adenoma or a fatty liver darkens on the opposed-phase image.
  • Amount matters. Set both amounts equal and the opposed-phase signal goes to zero: a voxel that is half fat and half water cancels completely. Make one species dominant and the dip becomes shallow. The India-ink outline around organs is this cancellation happening in the voxels that straddle a fat-water boundary.
  • Set Δf to zero and the beating stops: the sum is just A+B forever. Everything interesting here comes from the difference in frequency, not from the frequencies themselves, which is exactly why the rotating frame is a legitimate place to work.

Try this in class: switch between the 1.5 T and 3 T presets and watch the beat period halve. Chemical shift in hertz scales with field strength, which is why chemical-shift artifacts get worse, and Dixon echo times get shorter, as you go up in field.
