What this figure is for

Everything in MRI starts here. The net magnetisation M precesses about B0 at the Larmor frequency, and the only thing a receive coil can detect is the component turning in the transverse plane.

dM/dt = M × γB − (Mx, My, 0)/T2 − (Mz − M0)ẑ/T1

  • Why a rotating frame. At 3 T protons precess at about 128 MHz, which no animation can show. Sit on a carousel turning at that same rate and the precession vanishes: only the difference Δf between the spin and the carrier is left. Switch to the lab frame to see the fast motion that the carousel hides.
  • Excitation is just more precession. Turn on B1 and M precesses about the effective field instead. On resonance Beff lies in the transverse plane and M tips cleanly from z. Drag Δf away from zero and Beff tilts back toward z, so the same pulse produces a smaller, wobbling flip. That is the whole story of slice profile and off-resonance excitation.
  • Two independent clocks. T2 shrinks the transverse component; T1 regrows the longitudinal one. Set T2 to 20 ms and T1 to 3000 ms and watch the signal disappear long before M has returned to equilibrium. That gap is why we have to wait a TR.
  • Convention. A pulse applied along x′ rotates M from +z toward −y′. Spins with Δf > 0 precess anticlockwise seen from +z.

Try this in class: set T2 to 800 ms, play a 90°x pulse, then set Δf to 30 Hz. The vector now crawls around the transverse plane at exactly 30 revolutions per second of simulated time. Every spin in the body is doing this at a slightly different rate, which is the subject of the dephasing demos.
