MEG Simulator

A sibling to the EEG simulator and the PET scanner simulator. Describe a recording in a few lines, record the magnetic field of current dipoles in the head with SQUIDs in a helmet or with OPMs on the scalp, let the elevator, the traffic, the heart and the eyes in, and then do what an MEG lab does: shield, compensate, project, separate, filter, average and localize. The simulator always knows the truth, so every answer can be checked.

Working
The idea: a field a billion times weaker than the Earth's

When tens of thousands of pyramidal cells in a patch of cortex receive synaptic input at the same moment, the currents flowing inside their apical dendrites add up to a current dipole of a few tens of nanoampere-metres. Its magnetic field leaves the head undistorted by the skull and is about 100 femtotesla a few centimetres away: a billionth of the Earth's field, and a millionth of the field of a car passing in the street. Measuring it takes the most sensitive detectors there are, SQUIDs, coupled to pickup coils in a helmet; liquid helium in a dewar to keep them superconducting; a magnetically shielded room to keep the world out; and head position coils and a digitizer to know where the head is. Optically pumped magnetometers (OPMs) are a newer way to do the same thing at room temperature, on the scalp. Click a part of the system, or a stage below, to see how it works.

Pyramidal cells and the current dipole they form animated; current flows down the apical dendrites

A fold of cortex. Pyramidal cells stand perpendicular to the cortical surface, so on the crown of a gyrus they point at the scalp and on the wall of a sulcus they lie along it. An excitatory synapse on the apical dendrite lets positive charge in; it flows down inside the cell (the primary current, blue arrows) and returns through the surrounding tissue (the volume current, grey). Seen from a few centimetres away the loop is a current dipole, and its magnetic field circles the primary current (dots: field out of the page, crosses: into it).

Magnetometer field map and EEG potential map of the active patch, from the same head model as the rest of the simulator. Synchronous cells add linearly; asynchronous ones add like a random walk, as the square root of their number. A radial patch on a gyral crown is nearly invisible to MEG; two opposed sulcal walls cancel for both methods.

The head: primary and volume currents why the skull blurs EEG and leaves MEG alone

A cross-section through a tangential dipole in the four-shell head: brain, cerebrospinal fluid, skull and scalp. Grey lines are the volume currents, computed for these conductivities; colour is the potential they set up. The skull conducts tens of times worse than the brain, so the current spreads sideways before crossing it and the scalp potential is smeared. In a spherical head the magnetic field outside does not depend on the conductivities at all: the volume currents' contribution is fixed by the primary current alone (Sarvas, 1987).

Pickup coils and SQUIDs a superconducting flux transformer carries the field to the SQUID

Pickup coil

How each coil's response to a small magnetic source falls with distance, relative to a source 4 cm away. A magnetometer's falls as the cube of distance; a gradiometer subtracts two nearly equal readings of anything far away and its response falls one power faster. That is the gradiometer's distance filter: the brain is near, the world is far.

A DC SQUID is a superconducting ring broken by two Josephson junctions. Biased with a small current, the voltage across it oscillates with the magnetic flux through the ring, once per flux quantum, Φ0 = h/2e = 2.07 × 10-15 Wb. The electronics feed a current back into a coil beside the ring to hold the flux at the steepest point of the curve (the flux-locked loop); the feedback current is the output, linear over many flux quanta.

When the flux changes faster than the loop can follow, the SQUID passes through a whole period of its curve before the feedback catches up, and the loop locks again one flux quantum away. The output jumps by a fixed step and stays there: a flux jump or SQUID jump. Strong, fast fields (no shielded room, a nearby magnet) make them common.

The dewar 4 kelvin inside, 300 kelvin outside, two centimetres apart

SQUIDs and pickup coils are niobium, superconducting below 9 K, so they sit in liquid helium at 4.2 K inside a vacuum-insulated vessel shaped into a helmet at the bottom. A whole-head dewar holds about 70 litres and boils off several litres a day; many labs now recycle the gas. The price of the cold is distance: vacuum and insulation keep the coils about 2 cm from the scalp on top of the head and more where the head does not fill the helmet.

The magnetically shielded room mu-metal for slow fields, aluminium for fast ones

Layers of mu-metal, a nickel-iron alloy of very high permeability, give slowly varying fields an easier path around the room than through it. A layer of aluminium adds eddy-current shielding, which grows with frequency. Everything that must cross the walls does so through filtered connectors and waveguides; stimuli arrive by light, air tubes and plastic, and the participant is watched by camera. Opening the door, even an elevator next door, shows in the data.

Head position coils and the digitizer tying the head to the sensors and to the MRI

Before the recording a 3D digitizer pen records three anatomical landmarks (the nasion and the two preauricular points), which define the head coordinate frame, the positions of four or five small head position indicator (HPI) coils taped to the scalp, and a hundred or more points on the scalp and the nose. In the helmet, each coil is driven with its own frequency, above the brain's band, and its field is fitted as a magnetic dipole: the coils' positions in the helmet give the head's position, continuously if the coils stay on.

Head position over this recording, as the HPI coils would track it (switch on Head movement on the Noise tab or in the program). With SQUIDs the helmet stays put and the head moves inside it, so every source's field map slides across the sensors.

Optically pumped magnetometers laser light, an alkali vapour, and zero field

Circularly polarized laser light pumps the spins of rubidium atoms in a small heated glass cell into one direction, and a pumped vapour stops absorbing the light. Any magnetic field makes the spins precess away and the cell absorbs more, so the light reaching the photodiode measures the field. The effect is sharpest near zero field: within about a nanotesla. That is why OPMs need a well-shielded room and coils that null the remaining field, and why moving them through a gradient of a few nanotesla per metre makes artifacts.

A recording is a description

Every MEG study can be written down as a handful of decisions: which sensors, how fast to sample, what the online filters do, which shielding and noise rejection to use, what the participant does and when, and what is in and around the head. The program on the left is exactly that, in a small language. Change a line and press Build recording: the simulator records a new session and every other tab updates.       Two maps of one source
      
    The current inside the cells (the primary current) drives volume currents through the whole head. EEG measures the
        potential those volume currents set up on the scalp; MEG measures the magnetic field of both kinds of current. In a spherical
        head the field outside depends only on the primary current's position and its tangential part: a radial dipole makes no
        field outside at all, and a deep one makes very little, because the field falls with distance and the radial share grows toward
        the centre. The EEG potential map of a tangential dipole has its two poles on either side of the dipole, along it; the magnetic
        field circles the current, so the MEG map's two poles lie across it, rotated by 90 degrees. The skull smears the potential but
        not the field. Move the dipole below and watch both maps.

      Every sensor records every source
      
    The field of every source in the head reaches every sensor, so each channel records a weighted sum of all of them. The
        weights, one column per source and one row per sensor, form the mixing matrix A in x = A s. They depend on where
        each source is, its depth and its orientation, and on where the sensors are; for MEG, unlike EEG, not on the conductivities.
        Two sources whose field maps overlap are hard to tell apart, from the sensors and from any inverse method. Select a source to
        move it and watch its map, the mixing matrix and the recording change together.

      The brain is the smallest thing in the room
      
    An evoked field of a few hundred femtotesla has to be measured next to the Earth's 50 microtesla, field changes of tens of
        nanotesla from moving steel and currents in the building, the mains, the heart's picotesla and the eyes and muscles. No single
        trick removes all of it. The shielded room takes out most of the world; gradiometers ignore what
        is far; reference sensors measure the remaining field and subtract it; signal space projection
        removes the directions in sensor space that noise occupies; signal space separation splits the field into a part
        from inside the helmet and a part from outside and keeps only the inside. Each step costs something. Switch them on one at a time
        and watch the budget below.

      Frequency: rhythms, noise floors and what the shielding leaves
      
    MEG spectra are usually drawn as an amplitude spectral density, in femtotesla per root hertz, because that is how sensor noise
        is specified: a good SQUID has a flat floor of a few fT/√Hz with a rise at the lowest frequencies. Brain activity falls
        roughly as 1/f above that floor and rhythms stand out as peaks: alpha near 10 Hz over the back of the head with the eyes closed,
        beta near 20 Hz over the motor strip. Line noise is a needle at 50 or 60 Hz and its harmonics; the environment and the heart
        crowd the lowest frequencies. A time-frequency map shows when power changes: alpha appearing as the eyes close, or beta
        dropping after a stimulus and rebounding.

      Processing changes the data; know what each step does
      
    After the noise rejection come the steps an analyst chooses. Filters remove what you do not want and distort what you do, more
        so the steeper and closer to the signal they are, and a filter that does not shift latencies must look into the future. ICA can
        separate the heart, the eyes and a bad sensor from the brain only to the extent that they have fixed field patterns and
        independent time courses. Head movement blurs every average; with an array that supports SSS the field can be rebuilt as if the
        head had stayed still. The Recording, Spectra and Averaging tabs all show the output of this pipeline.

      Why averaging works, and what it costs
      
    An evoked field of a hundred femtotesla is buried in hundreds of femtotesla of everything else. If the response is the same on
        every trial and the rest is not time-locked to the stimulus, averaging N trials leaves the response untouched and shrinks the rest by
        a factor of √N. Signal-to-noise ratio grows as √N, so doubling it costs four times the trials. The catch is in the
        assumptions: if the latency wanders from trial to trial the average is smaller and wider than any single response, and artifacts that
        are time-locked or huge survive. Epochs are cut around each event, baseline corrected, screened for artifacts, and then averaged.

      Forward and inverse problems
      
    The forward problem, from a known source to the field at the sensors, has exactly one answer, and physics supplies
        it. The inverse problem, from the field back to the sources, has infinitely many: Helmholtz showed in 1853 that
        infinitely many current distributions inside a conductor produce the same field outside. Every inverse method adds an assumption to
        pick one. Dipole fitting assumes one or a few point sources. Minimum norm spreads the current over
        the whole brain and picks the distribution with the least total power. A beamformer assumes the sources are
        uncorrelated in time. Before any of that the head must be put in the same frame as an MRI, because a source is only as well placed on
        the anatomy as the coregistration that put it there.

    How to use this in class
    
      
    The tabs follow the life of an MEG study. The Instrument tab is the hardware, from a patch of cortex to a SQUID in
        liquid helium. The Experiment tab is the study, written as a short program; everything else is computed from it.
        MEG & EEG fields is the physics of one dipole, and Sources the sources of this recording.
        Noise & shielding is what MEG labs spend most of their effort on. Recording,
        Spectra, Processing and Averaging are the analysis; Source estimation
        runs the problem backwards. The concept boxes at the top of each tab open and close, so a projected screen can stay uncluttered.
    
      
    Things worth trying
    
      
      
        
    • The 90° rotation. On MEG & EEG fields, leave the dipole tangential and compare the two maps: the
          EEG poles lie along the dipole, the MEG poles across it. The third map draws the MEG field as contour lines over the EEG colours.
      • 
        
    • Radial and deep sources. Tilt the dipole slowly from 90° to 0°. The MEG map fades to nothing while the EEG
          map turns from a dipolar to a monopolar pattern. Then push a tangential dipole deeper: MEG loses more than EEG, the gradiometers
          most of all.
      • 
        
    • The skull. Change the brain to skull conductivity ratio: the EEG map widens and shrinks, the MEG map does not move.
      • 
        
    • What a sensor sees. In the last panel of the same tab, compare the sensitivity of a magnetometer, a planar
          gradiometer and an OPM at the same site, and of an EEG electrode.
      • 
        
    • The world outside. Load No shielded room. The traces are hundreds of picotesla of elevator and traffic.
          On the Noise tab, switch to axial gradiometers, then turn the room on. Watch the ladder and the budget.
      • 
        
    • Inside versus outside. Load Dental work and tSSS. Plain SSS removes the environment but keeps the dental
          artifact, because it is inside the helmet; temporal SSS removes it too.
      • 
        
    • ICA. In the Noise zoo, run ICA, find the heart, the blinks, the eye movements and the bad sensor from their
          maps and spectra, remove them and check with "Reveal".
      • 
        
    • √N. In the Teaching tour, press Play on the Averaging tab and watch the M100 appear out of the noise.
          Deviants are only 15% of trials, so their average stays noisier than the standards' throughout.
      • 
        
    • Head movement. Load Head movement and compensation; compare the three maps in the last panel of the
          Processing tab.
      • 
        
    • The template head. On Source estimation, hover over the MRI: every point is named from the CerebrA
          atlas, with its MNI coordinates. The built-in sources sit on the regions they are named for: Heschl's gyrus, the two walls of the
          central sulcus at the hand knob, the cuneus above the calcarine sulcus.
      • 
        
    • Coregistration. On Source estimation, press "Nasion 5 mm too high". The error tilts the head about the
          ears: the auditory source moves 5 mm on the anatomy and the edge of the brain up to a centimetre. Refine with the head shape: ICP
          brings both back to about a millimetre, but the worst error grows to about 6 mm if the head shape leaves out the nose.
      • 
        
    • Three answers. With the left auditory cortex, all three inverse methods agree within a few millimetres, though
          minimum norm lights up tens of cubic centimetres. Choose the deep source and switch to plain minimum norm: its peak jumps 3 cm
          toward the surface, while sLORETA and the beamformer, each normalized against that pull, stay within half a centimetre. Lower the
          trials to 10 and watch the dipole's confidence volume grow.
      • 
        
    • Correlated sources. Choose both auditory cortices and set the correlation to 1. One dipole explains less than half
          of the field, wherever it sits; fitting two finds both. The beamformer's map goes flat: each source's filter cancels the other. The
          same happens with this recording's average in the Teaching tour, whose two auditory cortices share a waveform.
      • 
      
    
      
    What the simulation computes
    
      
      
        
    • Anatomy. The MNI ICBM152 2009c nonlinear symmetric template, the average T1-weighted scan of 152 young adults,
          with the CerebrA atlas, at 1.5 mm. Its nasion and preauricular points, its 10-10 electrode positions, its eyes and the sites of the
          built-in generators were measured on it by tools/build_template.py; the source grid is its grey and white matter, the
          digitizer and ICP use its scalp, and the drawings of the head follow its outline. Sources and licences are in
          data/README.md.
      • 
        
    • Head and fields. A sphere fitted to the template's scalp above the ears, as a lab fits one to the digitized head
          shape, with the template shrunk 5% so its radius is 9.2 cm: brain 8.0 cm, CSF 8.2, skull 8.7, scalp 9.2 cm. Depths on the sliders
          are measured from this sphere; the real scalp is about a centimetre nearer over the temples and farther at the forehead and the
          back of the head. Magnetic fields outside come
          from the Sarvas formula, exact for any spherically layered head; EEG potentials from the four-shell series solution. Inside the
          head the field is the primary current's Biot-Savart field plus the volume currents' field from the Geselowitz surface integral.
      • 
        
    • Sensors. 105 sites on an ellipsoidal helmet, 1.7 to 4.7 cm from the template's scalp (2.2 cm over the
          vertex). Magnetometers are one coil; axial gradiometers two coaxial coils 5 cm apart; the planar array has a magnetometer and two orthogonal planar gradiometers (16.8 mm
          baseline) at each site; OPMs sit 6 mm above the scalp and move with the head. Gradiometers have small random imbalances.
          Nine reference magnetometers sit high in the dewar.
      • 
        
    • Noise. Sensor noise is white with a 1/f rise; brain background is fifty random cortical dipoles with 1/f time
          courses. The environment is magnetic dipoles metres to kilometres away (an elevator, traffic, the city), the mains a dipole a few
          metres away with harmonics, the heart a magnetic dipole in the chest; the eyes and muscles are current dipoles. The shielded room
          attenuates outside fields by a factor rising from 500 at DC to about 60 000 above 100 Hz.
      • 
        
    • Noise rejection. Reference compensation fits a uniform field and its five gradients to the references. SSP
          projects out the leading eigenvectors of the empty-room covariance or of averaged artifacts. SSS is the multipole expansion of
          Taulu and Kajola (inside order 8, outside order 3), tSSS removes inside time courses that also appear outside, and movement
          compensation rebuilds the inside field at the starting head position every second.
      • 
        
    • Inverse. A 6 mm grid in the brain with two tangential orientations per point; data whitened with the baseline
          noise covariance in the brain's signal subspace. Dipole fitting is a grid search refined by Nelder-Mead (for two dipoles,
          alternating searches from several starting points), with a 95% confidence volume from the chi-square of the residual; minimum norm
          follows MNE's conventions for depth weighting, dSPM and sLORETA; the beamformer is an LCMV filter with the neural activity index,
          built from the single trials between 50 and 300 ms.
      • 
        
    • Coregistration. Horn's closed-form rigid fit to the three landmarks, then iterative closest points between the
          digitized head shape and the MRI's scalp, with the landmarks kept in as three more points.
      • 
      
    
      
    What it does not model
    
      
      
        
    • Conduction in a real head. The anatomy is a real head's, but the fields come from the sphere fitted to it, so the boundary
          element models of real localization are not needed here; spheres are the classroom model and they get the main ideas right.
      • 
        
    • Extended sources. Every generator is a point dipole; the cancellation within folded patches appears only on the Instrument tab.
      • 
        
    • The SQUID electronics in detail, crosstalk between channels, and the fine structure of real shielded rooms and real OPM arrays.
      • 
        
    • Neural dynamics. Sources are waveforms chosen to look like MEG, not the output of a model of neurons.
      • 
        
    • One person's brain. The MRI is a template, an average of 152 brains, so it is smoother than any one scan and its folds are the
          average folds; its labels are as good as the CerebrA atlas's registration to it. Opened from the disk rather than a web server, the
          page cannot load it, and a synthetic head drawn around the sphere stands in.
      •
